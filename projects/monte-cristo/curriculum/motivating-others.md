# Motivating Others

## The Core Principle
- People act from their own reasons, not yours. Motivation lands when the ask connects to something they already care about — their identity, a goal they've stated, their standing with a group — not when you supply your own reasons why they should.
- External pressure (guilt, obligation, "you should") produces a short burst of compliance and no follow-through. An internal reason holds because it's theirs, not borrowed.

## Read the Person First
- Different people move on different levers: competition, belonging to the group, a concrete goal they've already named, or being trusted with real responsibility. The same line doesn't land the same way twice.
- Same discipline as `reading-signals.md`'s match-then-escalate logic — read what actually moves this specific person before pushing, rather than defaulting to your own favorite lever.

## Techniques

### Speak to identity, not instruction
"You're the guy who finishes what he starts" lands harder than "you should finish this" — it ties the action to who they already believe they are, rather than adding an external rule to follow.

### Make the ask small and concrete
Vague pushes ("keep going," "try harder") give nothing to actually do. "One more round" or "climb the rope once more" is specific and doable right now — specificity is what turns intention into action, same principle as `conversation-and-wit.md`'s point about specific questions being easier to answer than broad ones.

### Lead by doing, not describing
If you want someone moving, be visibly already moving yourself. Telling someone to have energy while standing still yourself is the fastest way to be ignored.

### Praise the specific thing, not the general effort
"That last rep was sharper than the one before" is believable and repeatable. "Good job" is generic enough to be ignored — it doesn't tell them what to keep doing.

### The ask itself — direct beats hinting
Same rule as escalating a conversation: state plainly what you want them to do. "I need you to hold this together for one more round" beats a vague "come on, let's go."

## Rallying a Group
- Shared language — "we," not "you" — makes the stakes collective rather than a lecture aimed at them individually.
- Name the stakes plainly and briefly. A long speech dilutes urgency; a short, clear line at the right moment lands harder than a paragraph.
- One clear instruction beats three vague ones — a group under pressure can execute one thing well, not a list.

## What Kills Motivation
- **Empty hype** — enthusiasm with no real reason behind it reads as noise, and people stop listening to it after the first few times.
- **Stacking too many asks at once** — overwhelm stalls action; one small, doable thing beats a list.
- **Leaning on guilt or fear** — works once. On repeat it's recognized as manipulation, and the relationship (and the willingness to be motivated by you again) takes the damage.

## Worked Example
Setting: corner between rounds, up on the scorecards but visibly gassed, hands dropping.

> "Hands up. Look at me — you're up on every card, he knows it, that's why he's rushing you. One more round of your jab and this is over. You're the guy who finishes rounds strong, not the guy who fades in the championship rounds. One more."

Notice the shape: names the actual situation (up on the cards), gives a specific and small ask (one more round of the jab, not "fight harder"), ties it to identity ("the guy who finishes strong"), and stays short enough to say in the time available between rounds.

## Debrief Questions
1. What did you actually say — did it name something they already cared about, or was it generic encouragement?
2. Was the ask specific and doable right now, or vague ("try harder," "come on")?
3. Did you lead by visibly doing it yourself, or only instruct from the sideline?
